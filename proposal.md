# Policy Proposal: Managing AI-Driven Disruption of Entry-Level Knowledge Work in Australia

**To:** Deputy Secretary, Employment and Skills, Australian Government Department of Employment and Workplace Relations  
**From:** Policy Analyst  
**Date:** 14th May 2026  

## Executive Summary

Generative artificial intelligence (Gen AI) is beginning to reshape Australia’s labour market by changing the tasks that sit inside jobs, especially in entry-level knowledge work. Junior roles in administration, customer service, accounting, marketing, legal support and software support often include routine writing, summarising, data handling and communication tasks that Gen AI can now perform or assist with. The main policy challenge is not mass unemployment today, but the risk that early-career workers lose the basic tasks through which they normally build skills and progress. This proposal recommends a three-part policy package: targeted AI transition training, temporary wage and mobility support, and responsible AI adoption grants for small and medium enterprises (SMEs). This package would protect vulnerable workers while still allowing Australia to capture productivity gains from AI.

## Problem Definition and Context

Gen AI is changing work mainly by changing tasks, not simply by removing whole occupations. For example, a junior analyst may still have the same job title, but AI may now draft first versions of reports, summarise documents, prepare spreadsheets and generate client emails. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) explain that automation changes the “task content” of production by shifting some tasks from labour to capital, creating both displacement effects and productivity effects. This means the key policy question is not whether AI will “take all jobs”, but which tasks will be automated, which workers will be complemented, and whether new tasks are created quickly enough for labour to remain valuable.

Australia should treat entry-level knowledge work as a priority because these jobs are important stepping stones into professional careers. For example, junior office workers, graduates and assistants often learn through routine but valuable tasks such as drafting, checking, researching and customer communication. Jobs and Skills Australia (2025) notes that entry-level roles could be disproportionately affected because Gen AI is well suited to standardised and repetitive tasks often performed by junior staff. This matters because if these tasks disappear without replacement learning pathways, young workers may find it harder to gain experience, even if total employment does not immediately fall.

## Analysis of Labour Market Impacts
Task transformation is the first labour-market impact of Gen AI, which in many routine cognitive tasks can write, classify, summarize and respond to information faster than a human. The International Labour Organisation (ILO) (2025) states that clerical occupations will have the highest level of exposure to Gen AI, however, digitised professional or technical occupations are also becoming increasingly exposed. The ILO also points out that since most occupations still require human input it is more likely that we will undergo job transformation, rather than the total replacement of jobs. This indicates that the Australian policy should be aimed at assisting workers redesign their jobs, rather than just preparing workers for unemployment.
The second way AI affects how we work is by creating an imbalance in the productivity increase across workers. A person who is proficient at using AI for tasks may now be able to produce a higher amount of output in a shorter time frame (e.g. helping customers solve problems) than a worker who does not have this technology available to them. For example, Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (2025) demonstrated that using a generative AI assistant increased the productivity of customer service agents by approximately 15% (on average), but that the greatest benefit was to those workers who had less experience or lower skill levels. As such, this finding adds to the evidence that AI can serve as a supplement to the existing workforce as opposed to being solely a replacement for the existing workforce. However, these benefits are dependent upon the implementation of adequate training programs, proper workplace design, and the provision of supervision by human workers.

The third impact of AI on working conditions is to increase the pressure on wages and the quality of jobs. If junior workers lose their jobs due to the automation of their tasks by AI, employers will have less need to hire additional workers or assume that fewer workers will produce the same amount of work output. Acemoglu and Restrepo (2019) have argued that automation will decrease the share of value added to labour when the effect of displacing workers is greater than that of creating additional low-skilled jobs. Continuing with an example used above, if a law firm employs an AI tool to complete basic legal draft documents, they would require significantly fewer paralegals unless those paralegals are assigned to assist with the review of legal draft documents, assist with client service or assist with compliance work. This suggests that unless there is sufficient policy support provided for worker retraining and bargaining power outcomes, productivity gains will not necessarily convert into wage gains for workers.

Inequality in adoption of Artificial Intelligence between firms is the fourth impact. Large organisations will generally adopt AI technologies more quickly than small businesses due to their greater ability to generate and manage data; have greater financial resources; have more technical personnel available; and have greater management capacity.. This can sometimes lead small businesses to only make passive AI adoption through software updates without redesigning work processes or adequately training their workforce., (Productivity Commission,2024) suggests that in order for AI to have productivity gains, organisations must have complementary investments made into things like training, organisational redesign and organisational process improvements., If there was no support for effective policy responses, AI has significant potential to increase the productivity disparity between large firms which can reinvent work processes through AI development and deployment, compared to small and medium-sized enterprises which lack the capacity to safely and productively employ AI.

The fifth impact of Gen AI on the workforce are inequity in the potential risk exposure of different groups of employees. Women, people living with a disability, First Nation peoples, older workers and workers who have lower digital access may be at different levels of risk depending upon their respective occupations and access to workforce development training (Jobs & Skills Australia, 2025). This means there will not be equal exposure to the opportunities from the evolution of Gen AI by employee group: there will be a small number of job types where higher concentrations of occupationally clustered employees will be at a higher risk due to there being gaps in access to digital technologies. Therefore, as part of any effective policy response to the evolution of Gen AI, equity should be included in addition to productivity.

## Policy Options and Evaluation

## Policy Options and Evaluation

The first option is a National AI Transition Training Program for workers in entry-level knowledge roles. The program would provide short courses in AI literacy, digital skills and human-AI collaboration through TAFE and universities. This option has strong long-term benefits because it helps workers adapt to changing tasks and supports future productivity. However, it would require significant coordination and funding across government and education providers.

The second option is wage insurance and mobility support for workers affected by AI disruption. This could provide temporary financial support while workers retrain or move into new roles. The main advantage is that it protects workers during transition and reduces financial pressure. However, it does not directly address long-term skill gaps unless combined with retraining programs.

The third option is responsible AI adoption grants for SMEs. Businesses would receive support to adopt AI systems if they also provide worker training and maintain human oversight. This option could improve productivity and help smaller firms remain competitive. However, the program would require monitoring to ensure public funding is used responsibly.

## Recommended Policy Package and Implementation Plan

The recommended approach is a combined AI Labour Transition Package that includes training, wage support and responsible SME AI adoption grants. This approach is stronger than relying on a single policy because workers need new skills, businesses need support to adopt AI effectively, and displaced workers need short-term protection. A balanced package would help Australia improve productivity while reducing inequality and protecting entry-level career pathways.

Implementation should begin by identifying occupations where entry-level tasks are highly exposed to Gen AI, particularly in administrative, customer-service and junior analytical roles. Jobs and Skills Australia (2025) argues that a targeted approach is important because the effects of AI will not be experienced equally across industries or worker groups. Early pilot programs should focus on AI literacy, digital communication and human-AI collaboration through TAFE and university partnerships.

The government should also provide temporary wage and mobility support for workers affected by AI disruption while expanding SME grants linked to worker training and human oversight. The Productivity Commission (2024) states that AI productivity gains are strongest when businesses combine technology adoption with workforce capability development. This means support should focus not only on purchasing AI tools, but also on helping workers adapt to changing tasks.

Success should be measured through outcomes such as course completion, employment retention and wage recovery rather than enrolment numbers alone. A practical evaluation framework would help ensure public funding supports real labour-market adjustment and long-term workforce resilience.
